April 1982. Dan Shechtman is looking through an electron microscope at a cooled metal alloy. The atoms form a pattern with ten-fold symmetry. Every textbook says that is impossible.

He checks again. And again. The pattern never changes. Crystals can only repeat in certain ways, and this one breaks the rule.

He tells his colleagues. They laugh. His boss hands him a textbook and tells him to read it. Then he is asked to leave his research group.

The most famous chemist alive, Linus Pauling, says it out loud: "There is no such thing as quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists."

But Shechtman refuses to back down. Other labs repeat his experiment, and the pattern shows up everywhere.

In 2011, he walks onto a stage in Stockholm and receives the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The impossible crystal was real all along.

1920. Robert Goddard has an idea. A rocket can fly into space, because it pushes against its own exhaust, not against the air.

The New York Times disagrees. Its editorial says Goddard lacks the basic knowledge taught in high school. Rockets cannot work in a vacuum, it says.

The whole country laughs. Goddard moves his tests to a quiet farm in Massachusetts, so nobody can watch him fail.

March 16, 1926. His rocket, the first ever to burn liquid fuel, lifts off. It flies for only two and a half seconds. But it flies.

Goddard dies in 1945, still unknown to most of the world.

Then, July 1969. Apollo 11 is flying to the Moon, through the vacuum of space, on rockets.

Three days before landing, the New York Times prints a correction. It says it is now definitely established that a rocket can function in a vacuum.

It took them 49 years.

Next story.

1940s. Barbara McClintock is staring at corn kernels, and the colors make no sense. Some are spotted. Some are streaked.

Her explanation is wild. Genes are not fixed in place. They move. They jump from one spot on a chromosome to another, switching other genes on and off.

Every scientist believes genes stay put, like beads on a string.

In 1951, she presents her results. The room goes quiet. Nobody understands her, and nobody believes her. Years of work, and they treat her like she made it up.

So she stops fighting. She keeps growing her corn and says nothing more.

Then, decades later, other scientists find the same jumping genes in bacteria, and in humans.

In 1983, at age 81, Barbara McClintock wins the Nobel Prize in Medicine. By herself.

She had been right for more than thirty years.

Next story.

1930. A nineteen-year-old student is sailing from India to England. On the ship, he does the math on how stars die.

His answer is shocking. If a dying star is heavy enough, it cannot settle down quietly. It keeps collapsing. This is the first step toward the idea of black holes.

January 1935. Chandrasekhar presents it in London. In the audience sits Arthur Eddington, the most famous astronomer in the world.

Eddington stands up. He mocks the idea in front of everyone, and calls it nonsense. Nobody defends the young man.

Chandrasekhar is crushed. He leaves for America and moves on to other work.

But the math is right, and the data slowly proves it. Today the limit still carries his name.

In 1983, Chandrasekhar wins the Nobel Prize in Physics.

The boy from the ship was right the whole time.

Next story.

1747. A British navy ship is sailing, and the sailors are dying. Their gums bleed. Their teeth fall out. Their old wounds open. It is scurvy, and it kills more sailors than enemy guns.

Surgeon James Lind tries something nobody has tried. He splits twelve sick men into six pairs. Each pair gets a different treatment: vinegar, seawater, cider and others.

Two men get oranges and lemons.

Within days, they are on their feet, helping to nurse the others. The rest are still sick.

Lind has proof. He writes it all down and publishes it.

And the navy does nothing. For decades. The doctors argue and the officers shrug.

Only in 1795, forty-eight years after the experiment, does the navy start issuing lemon juice to every sailor.

Scurvy nearly vanishes from the fleet.

The cure was sitting in a ship's kitchen the whole time.
