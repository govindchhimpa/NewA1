Nineteen twenty-five. A young woman at Radcliffe College hands in her PhD thesis, and it is about to change astronomy. Her name is Cecilia Payne.

At that time, almost every astronomer believes the stars are made of the same stuff as the Earth.
But Payne studies starlight with a new idea about how heat changes atoms. The different lines in a star's light do not mean different ingredients. They mean different temperatures.

So she corrects for the heat, and the answer is astonishing. Stars are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium.

Then the most powerful astronomer in America, Henry Norris Russell, tells her it cannot be right.

So Payne does something quiet. She writes in her thesis that her results are almost certainly not real. But she keeps every number exactly as it was.

Four years later, in nineteen twenty-nine, Russell publishes a paper with the same answer. For years, the credit goes mostly to him.

But in nineteen fifty-six, Payne becomes the first woman at Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences promoted to full professor from within. And astronomer Otto Struve calls her thesis the most brilliant ever written in astronomy.

She was told her answer was wrong, so she wrote down that it was wrong. The numbers were right.
