Nineteen twenty-five. A twenty-five year old woman hands in her PhD thesis, and it is about to change astronomy. Her name is Cecilia Payne.

She studies at Radcliffe College, because Harvard does not give doctorates to women. And she is looking at the light of the stars.

At that time, almost every astronomer believes the same thing. The Sun and the stars are made of the same stuff as the Earth. Iron, silicon, carbon, oxygen.

But Payne has a new tool. An idea from the Indian physicist Meghnad Saha, about how heat changes atoms. And it shows her that the old readings were misleading. The different lines in a star's light do not mean different ingredients. They mean different temperatures.

So she corrects for the heat. And the answer is astonishing. Stars are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. The heavier elements are only a small part, in amounts much like Earth's.

But then comes the problem. The most powerful astronomer in America, Henry Norris Russell, reads her work. He tells her it cannot be right. The Sun cannot be so different from the Earth. He calls her result spurious.

She has a choice.

So Payne does something quiet. She writes in her thesis that her results are almost certainly not real. But she keeps every calculation. Every number stays exactly as it was. And she hands it in.

Four years later, in nineteen twenty-nine, Russell publishes a paper of his own. Using different methods, he reaches the same answer. Hydrogen and helium dominate the stars. He mentions her work, calling it the most important earlier determination. But for many years, the credit goes mostly to him.

And Payne? Harvard will not make her a professor. For years she works in lower-paid research, and her courses do not even appear in the catalogue until nineteen forty-five.

Slowly, things change. In nineteen fifty-six she becomes the first woman at Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences promoted to full professor from within. Later, she becomes the first woman to lead a Harvard department.

And nearly forty years after her thesis, astronomer Otto Struve calls it the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy.

Today we know she was right. About three quarters of the ordinary matter in our galaxy is hydrogen, and about a quarter is helium.

She was told her answer was wrong, so she wrote down that it was wrong, and she left the numbers exactly as they were. The numbers were right.
