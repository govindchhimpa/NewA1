There is a country where the highest point is about four and a half meters above the sea. That is roughly the height of a double-decker bus.

This is Tuvalu.

Out in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, about halfway between Hawaii and Australia, nine tiny islands and atolls sit scattered across the water. All of them are made of coral.

Put together, they hold less land than half of Manhattan. And about ten thousand people call them home.

Most of them live on one atoll, Funafuti. On its main island, the land is so narrow that in places you can see the open ocean on one side and the calm lagoon on the other.

For generations, life here was simple. People fished, grew coconuts, and farmed a root crop called pulaka in pits dug into the ground. Rain on the roofs filled the tanks for drinking water.

Then the sea started to rise.

But here is the strange part. It does not only arrive over the beach. It arrives from underneath.

Coral rock is full of tiny holes, like a sponge. So when the ocean rises, the water pushes up through the ground itself.

At high tide, puddles appear in the middle of the island, far from any shore. Salt creeps into the soil, and the pulaka pits start to die. Salt seeps into the wells.

You cannot build a wall against water that comes from below.

And then come the king tides, the highest tides of the year, when the sea spills across the island.

In November twenty twenty-one, at the world climate summit in Glasgow, Tuvalu's foreign minister, Simon Kofe, recorded a speech. He stood in a suit, knee deep in seawater, on a spot that used to be dry land.

His message was simple. This is happening to us first, but it will not stop with us.

NASA scientists have projected that by twenty fifty, half of Funafuti could be underwater at high tide.

So Tuvalu began doing two things at once. It kept asking the world to cut emissions. And it started preparing for the worst.

In twenty twenty-three, Tuvalu and Australia signed a treaty called the Falepili Union. Australia promised to open its doors to two hundred and eighty Tuvaluans every year, a kind of climate visa, with the right to live, work, and study there.

When applications opened in twenty twenty-five, the response was huge. More than a third of the whole country applied.

Think about that. A third of a nation, ready to leave the only home their families have ever known.

Because a country is not only land. It is language, songs, ancestors, and the little roads where you learned to ride a bike.

Tuvalu is now trying to prove something no country has ever had to prove: that a nation can survive, even if its coastline does not.

So who wins this race, the rising sea, or the people trying to outrun it?
