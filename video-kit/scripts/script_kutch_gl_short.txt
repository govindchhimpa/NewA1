On a quiet evening in June eighteen nineteen, the ground in western India rose up, and a wall of earth appeared where there had been none. People called it the Dam of God.

This is the Rann of Kutch, beside the border with Pakistan. Today it is a flat, white desert of salt.

But back then, a branch of the Indus river, the Nara, flowed past a mud fort named Sindri and out to the sea.

June sixteenth. About a quarter to seven, the earth shakes for two or three minutes. It is around magnitude seven point seven, and at least fifteen hundred people die.

Then the land itself changes. A ridge about six meters high pushes up across eighty kilometers, right in the river's path. Around Sindri Fort, the ground sinks. The sea rushes in, and survivors climb to the top of the ruins.

A river country becomes a salty lake of more than a thousand square kilometers.

The new ridge gets a name. Allah Bund. The Dam of God.

The blocked Nara keeps pushing, and in time it cuts through the Allah Bund itself.

Mountains take millions of years to grow. The Dam of God took a few minutes.
