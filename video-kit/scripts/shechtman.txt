April 1982. Dan Shechtman is looking through an electron microscope at a cooled metal alloy. The atoms form a pattern with ten-fold symmetry. Every textbook says that is impossible.

He checks again. And again. The pattern never changes. Crystals can only repeat in certain ways, and this one breaks the rule.

He tells his colleagues. They laugh. His boss hands him a textbook and tells him to read it. Then he is asked to leave his research group.

The most famous chemist alive, Linus Pauling, says it out loud: "There is no such thing as quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists."

But Shechtman refuses to back down. Other labs repeat his experiment, and the pattern shows up everywhere.

In 2011, he walks onto a stage in Stockholm and receives the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The impossible crystal was real all along.
