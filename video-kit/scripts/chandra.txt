1930. A nineteen-year-old student is sailing from India to England. On the ship, he does the math on how stars die.

His answer is shocking. If a dying star is heavy enough, it cannot settle down quietly. It keeps collapsing. This is the first step toward the idea of black holes.

January 1935. Chandrasekhar presents it in London. In the audience sits Arthur Eddington, the most famous astronomer in the world.

Eddington stands up. He mocks the idea in front of everyone, and calls it nonsense. Nobody defends the young man.

Chandrasekhar is crushed. He leaves for America and moves on to other work.

But the math is right, and the data slowly proves it. Today the limit still carries his name.

In 1983, Chandrasekhar wins the Nobel Prize in Physics.

The boy from the ship was right the whole time.
