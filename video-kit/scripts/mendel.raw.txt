1856 A monk named Gregor Mendel plants peas in a monastery garden in Burno. He wants to know how traits pass from parents to children. For eight years, he grows and counts about 28,000 pea plants. Tall or short, smooth or wrinkled, the same patterns appear again and again. His discovery is simple. Traits are passed on in separate units, and they follow exact rules. In 1866, he publishes his results. Almost nobody reads them. The few who do do not understand. Mendel keeps working, and in 1884, he dies as a quiet abbot, mostly forgotten. Then, in 1900, three scientists each rediscover his rules on their own. They open the old paper and it is all there. 16 years after his death. Today, Mendel is called the father of genetics.
