1940s. Barbara McClintock is staring at corn kernels, and the colors make no sense. Some are spotted. Some are streaked.

Her explanation is wild. Genes are not fixed in place. They move. They jump from one spot on a chromosome to another, switching other genes on and off.

Every scientist believes genes stay put, like beads on a string.

In 1951, she presents her results. The room goes quiet. Nobody understands her, and nobody believes her. Years of work, and they treat her like she made it up.

So she stops fighting. She keeps growing her corn and says nothing more.

Then, decades later, other scientists find the same jumping genes in bacteria, and in humans.

In 1983, at age 81, Barbara McClintock wins the Nobel Prize in Medicine. By herself.

She had been right for more than thirty years.
